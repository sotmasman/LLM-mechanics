Outline

This lesson tries the interpretability methods of Lesson 5 and Lesson 7 on GPT-2, an open-weights model. What comes out of a forward pass

Before looking inside the model, look at what it returns. GPT-2 small, prompt "The capital of France is the city of", run with run_model (our MATLAB forward pass, model_forward).

1 · Input: tokens

The text is split into tokens and the model only ever sees their IDs (0-based; <|endoftext|> is put first):

Position012345678
Token<|endoftext|>The␣capital␣of␣France␣is␣the␣city␣of
ID50256464313928648813182621748286

␣ = a leading space, which is part of the token: " of" and "of" are different tokens.

2 · Output: one probability distribution per position

One forward pass returns a logit for every vocabulary token at every position: a 50,257 × 9 matrix here. A softmax turns each column into probabilities that sum to 1. The last column is the prediction for the token after the prompt; every earlier column is the model's guess at the token that comes next at that point, which is exactly what training scores.

3 · From residual stream to probabilities

At each position the last layer leaves a vector of 768 numbers, x(11) (the residual stream). Three steps turn it into probabilities:

StepFormulaSizeHere (last position, " of")
1 · Final layer normx̂ = γ ⊙ (x(11) − μ) / σ + β
μ, σ: mean and standard deviation of the 768 entries; γ, β: learned scale and shift (768 each); ⊙: element-by-element product (MATLAB .*)		768σ = 15.0
2 · Unembed: logitsz = WU x̂   i.e.  zt = E[t] · x̂
WU: 50,257 × 768; row E[t] is token t's output direction; GPT-2 uses each token's input embedding for it (tied weights). Vectors are columns here, as in our MATLAB code.		50,257z(" Paris") = 14.94
z(" Marse") = 14.86
3 · Softmax: probabilitiespt = ezt / Σj ezj
positive, sum to 1; only differences between logits matter		50,257p(" Paris") = 9.7%
p(" Marse") = 8.9%

A logit difference is a log probability ratio: z(" Paris") − z(" Marse") = 0.09 (from the unrounded logits), so p(" Paris") / p(" Marse") = e0.09 ≈ 1.09 (9.7 / 8.9). Adding the same number to every logit changes nothing, which is why a single logit has no meaning on its own. Sampling at temperature τ uses pt ∝ ezt/τ: τ < 1 sharpens the distribution, τ → 0 is greedy. The entropy H = −Σt pt ln pt measures how spread out it is: 5.69 nats here, against ln 50,257 = 10.8 for a uniform guess. Training lowers the loss, Loss = −ln p(actual next token), averaged over positions.

In our exported weights γ is folded into WU and β into a bias bU = WUβ, so the code computes z = WU (x(11) − μ) / σ + bU. The export also centres WU: each of its columns has its mean over the vocabulary subtracted. That shifts every logit at a position by the same amount, so the probabilities are unchanged, but the logits differ slightly from other GPT-2 implementations. The logit lens (tab 3) applies the same three steps to x(l) of an earlier layer; direct logit attribution (tab 4) splits step 2 into one share per component.

4 · Choosing the next token

" Paris" wins, but with only 9.7%: " Marse" (the start of " Marseille") is close at 8.9%, then other French cities. The model is not certain; it spreads probability over plausible continuations. Greedy decoding takes the top token; sampling draws from the distribution (the temperature sets how spread out it is).

5 · Generating text

Generation repeats the forward pass: append the chosen token, run again, choose again. Ten greedy steps (nNew = 10):

The capital of France is the city of Paris, and the capital of the French Republic.

Probabilities of the chosen tokens: 9.7%, 48%, 18%, 15%, 26%, 54%, 26%, 17%, 26%, 44%.

What each layer would predict

Tab 2 read the prediction at the end of the model. The logit lens reads it from the middle: take the residual stream after layer l and pass it straight to the output, as if the remaining layers were skipped (nostalgebraist 2020; Lesson 7, tab 2). Same model and prompt as tab 2: GPT-2 small, "The capital of France is the city of".

1 · The readout

p(l) = softmax(WU · LayerNorm(x(l)) + bU)

x(l) is the residual stream leaving layer l (768 numbers per position); LayerNorm, WU and bU are the model's own final layer norm and unembedding. At l = 11, the last layer, this is exactly the model's output from tab 2.

2 · The answer appears late

Up to layer 7, " Paris" is not in the top 250 (rank 4,969 at layer 0, 259 at layer 7); the top guess is the generic " the". City names arrive at layer 8 (" Paris" rank 5). At layers 9–10 the readout is confident, but in " Marse" (61–62%), with " Paris" at 28–32%. The last layer reverses the order and spreads the probability: " Paris" 9.7%, " Marse" 8.9%.

3 · Not a smooth build-up

The top token changes from layer to layer and confidence does not grow steadily: it can be highest in the middle and fall at the end (here 62% at layer 10, 10% at layer 11). Across positions (panel c), early layers often predict a common follower of the current word (" of" → " the") or the word itself (" capital" → " capital" up to layer 4).

Which components pushed the answer

The logit lens (tab 3) asks when the prediction appears. Direct logit attribution (DLA) asks who wrote it: it splits the final logit of a token into one share per component (Lesson 7, tab 3). Same model and prompt: GPT-2 small, "The capital of France is the city of", where " Paris" (9.7%) beat " Marse" (8.9%).

1 · The split

The residual stream at the end is a sum of what every component wrote:

x(11) = x(emb) + Σl Δxattn,l + Σl Δxmlp,l

The logit of token t is E[t] · LayerNorm(x(11)) + bU(t), with E[t] the token's row of WU. Holding LayerNorm's scale at its actual value makes this linear, so each term gets a share and the shares add up exactly to the logit.

2 · Two questions

logit(" Paris"): who raised " Paris" at all? Many components push all plausible cities up together.

logit(" Paris") − logit(" Marse"): who made " Paris" win? Only the difference matters for the choice, and it removes everything the two cities share.

"Direct" means only what a component writes straight to the output; effects passed on through later layers are not counted.

3 · Who raised " Paris"

Its logit, 14.94, is attention +7.72, MLPs +5.65, bU +1.59 and the embedding −0.01. Most of it comes late: layers 8–11 give 80% of the attention and MLP shares. The largest single share is head 9.8 (+3.10), which puts 76% of its attention on " France": it carries information from the country's position to the last position. MLP 10 adds +2.15.

4 · Who made " Paris" win

Almost no one. The difference is only +0.09, and the attention (−1.06) and MLP (−1.15) shares both favour " Marse"; head 9.8 too (−0.37), so it raises " Marse" even more than " Paris". " Paris" wins on bU (+2.33): a fixed term, the same for every prompt, that comes from the final layer norm's bias (folded into bU when the weights are exported).

Connection to tab 3: the flip from " Marse" (layers 9–10) to " Paris" (layer 11) is not written by layer 11, whose own share favours " Marse" (−0.37). Instead, layer 11 makes the residual stream much larger (scale 9.0 → 15.0), so after LayerNorm everything written earlier counts less, including the lead for " Marse" (−3.1 → −2.2 logits), while the fixed bU advantage of " Paris" (+2.33) stays. The same shrinking flattens the whole distribution (" Marse" 62% → 9%).

Comparing directions

Tabs 2–4 read vectors through the output layer. Cosine similarity compares two vectors directly: do they point the same way? It works for any pair of vectors in the model: token embeddings, residual-stream states, or a state and a token's output direction (Lesson 5, tab 4). Same model and prompt: GPT-2 small, "The capital of France is the city of".

1 · The measure

cos θ = (u · v) / (‖u‖ ‖v‖)

1 = same direction, 0 = orthogonal, −1 = opposite. The lengths are divided out, so only the direction counts. In 768 dimensions two random directions have a cosine near 0 (typically within ±0.04), so values like 0.2 are already far from chance.

2 · Words: related words point alike

Before any layer has run, words of the same category already share direction: 0.51 on average within a category, 0.26 between (Fig. 4; colours 0.64, cities 0.51). Every cosine is positive, though: the embeddings share a common component, so "unrelated" is around 0.2–0.3 here, not 0.

3 · Sentences: high everywhere

At the last position the France and Italy prompts are nearly identical (cosine 1.00 to two decimals) up to layer 7: the token there is " of" in both, and the country reaches it only at layers 8–9 (lowest 0.94; Japan 0.86). An unrelated sentence still scores 0.56–0.90, and 0.90 at the output (layer 11). Raw cosines between residual-stream states are compressed by a large direction they all share, so compare them with each other, not with 0.

4 · Stream and output directions: where the cosine enters the logit

Step 2 of tab 2, zt = E[t] · x̂, written with the angle (γ and β folded into WU and bU, as in our exported weights):

zt = √768 · ‖E[t]‖ · cos(x − μ, E[t]) + bU(t)

LayerNorm fixes the length of x − μ (‖x − μ‖ / σ = √768 = 27.7), so a token's logit depends only on its angle with the residual stream, its own length ‖E[t]‖ and its bias. The cosine is the only part that changes with the stream: for one token, the logit is the cosine rescaled and shifted. Between tokens the scale and shift differ, so a larger cosine does not mean a larger logit. Here ‖E[" Paris"]‖ = 4.11, ‖E[" Marse"]‖ = 4.90, ‖E[" the"]‖ = 2.62.

" Paris" vs " Marse" (Fig. 6): the last position turns toward the generic " the" right after layer 0; the two cities rise slowly together and overtake it only after layer 8, as in the logit lens (tab 3). After layer 9 the stream is in fact slightly closer in angle to " Paris" (0.232) than to " Marse" (0.216), yet the logit lens favours " Marse": its longer output vector (4.90 vs 4.11) outweighs the angle. At the output the angles are almost equal (0.117 vs 0.115), so the length and the bias bU decide, as in tab 4.

The arrangement of many sentences

Tab 5 compared two vectors at a time. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) looks at the arrangement of a whole set: for a set of sentences, which ones are close to which, and does that arrangement follow a category (Lesson 5, tab 4)? GPT-2 small, residual stream at the last word of each sentence (the word before the final "."), for 16 sentences, 4 in each of 4 topics.

1 · The RDM

At layer l, for every pair of sentences, the dissimilarity 1 − r, where r is the correlation between their two vectors (here the same as their cosine: our exported weights that write into the residual stream are centred, so every state has mean zero across its 768 units). For 16 sentences this is a 16 × 16 matrix, the representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM), with 120 distinct pairs. 0 = the same pattern; larger = more different.

2 · Compare with a model

A model RDM says what topic alone predicts: 0 for two sentences of the same topic, 1 for different topics. The layer's RDM is compared with it by the Spearman correlation ρ over the 120 pairs. Only ranks count, so the shared direction that squeezes raw cosines (tab 5) does not matter. With a two-valued model, ρ is proportional to the gap between the mean ranks of between- and within-topic pairs; even perfect separation gives only 0.69 (24 within vs 96 between pairs), the ceiling in Fig. 7e.

4 · How the panels fit together

There is one RDM per point (emb and layers 0–11): 13 matrices like panel a. Each other panel condenses them differently.

a shows one of them in full: layer 10, all 16 × 16 pairs.

b reduces every RDM to two numbers: the mean of its within-topic cells (inside the diagonal blocks of a, without the diagonal) and of its between-topic cells (outside the blocks). The layer-10 points of b are the two numbers in a's title, 0.19 and 0.23.

c compares whole RDMs with each other: cell (i, j) is the Spearman correlation between the 120 pairs of layer i's RDM and those of layer j's. Panel a is one of the 13 matrices compared; the diagonal of c is 1 (each RDM with itself).

e does what c does, but against the hand-made RDM in d instead of another layer: it is the row c would have if d were added as a 14th "layer". b and e both ask whether the arrangement follows topic; b in units of 1 − r (sensitive to the overall level), e in ranks (only the order of the pairs counts).

5 · Topic builds up late

Comparing each layer's RDM with the topic model (panel d) gives panel e. At the embedding the arrangement has almost nothing to do with topic (ρ = 0.05). Topic structure then grows steadily with depth, faster after layer 5, to 0.38 at layers 10–11, a bit over half the ceiling. Panel b shows why it stays modest: within- and between-topic pairs rise and fall together (the sentences spread apart over the first layers and converge again toward the output), and only the gap between them is topic. It grows from 0 at the embedding to about 0.05 at layer 9; at layer 10, 1 − r is 0.19 within vs 0.23 between (panel a).

6 · The arrangement drifts

Neighbouring layers arrange the sentences alike (panel c), and layers 2–8 form a block with similar arrangements. Further apart, the arrangement changes: layer 0's RDM has ρ 0.91 with layer 1's but 0.56 with layer 11's. The geometry is rebuilt gradually rather than kept (tab 8 finds the same for a single direction), and the last two layers rearrange the sentences most, as they turn to predicting the next token.

The largest directions, without labels

RSA (tab 6) compared the arrangement with a hypothesis, the topic model. Principal component analysis (PCA) uses no hypothesis and no labels: it finds the directions along which the states vary most, and the labels only colour the plot afterwards. As in Fernando & Guitchounts (2025; Lesson 8, tab 3), one PCA covers every sentence at every layer, so all layers share the same axes and each sentence becomes a path through layers 0–11. GPT-2 small, the residual stream at the last word of each sentence, three sets of sentences (card 1).

Sentiment is the easy contrast, passive role the hard one; tabs 8 and 9 test both with labelled readouts.

2 · One PCA for all layers

Stack the states of all sentences at layers 0–11 (16–48 sentences × 12 layers), subtract their overall mean, and find the principal components (PCs): the orthogonal directions of largest variance. Two preparations: each layer is first scaled to the same average length (the stream grows about 100-fold with depth, and the last layers would otherwise dominate), and the embedding is left out (it differs from every later state, and PC1 would just be the step from emb to layer 0).

3 · Shared path or differences?

A PC can capture depth (every sentence moves along it together, layer by layer) or differences between sentences at the same layer. For each PC, the share of its variance that lies between sentences at the same layer tells which; run_PCA prints it for every PC. The PCs are found without labels, so a PC with differences between sentences need not follow any of our groups.

4 · How far apart are the groups?

At each layer, in the full (scaled) residual stream, not just the plotted PCs: the mean distance between the group means, divided by the mean distance of a sentence from its own group mean. About 1: the groups differ as much as single sentences vary; well below 1: they overlap. This is the one step that uses the labels.

5 · Depth comes first; topics show

In all three sets, PC1 (20–25% of the variance) is the path every sentence shares: its scores follow the layer (correlation 0.94–0.97 with depth) and hardly differ between sentences. Most of the next PCs hold differences between sentences. For topic these differences follow the labels without being told to: already on PCs 1–3 the four topic paths take different routes, weather the most distinct (panel a), and the groups move apart with depth, from a ratio of 0.86 at layer 0 to 1.15 at layer 11 (panel c), as RSA found in tab 6.

6 · Sentiment hides, roles are absent

The two sentiment groups follow almost the same path (panel d), and their means are only about 0.2 apart against a spread of about 0.5 within each group (ratio 0.34–0.42, panel f), at every layer. Yet difference in means reads sentiment at 0.96 (tab 8). The difference is real but lies along one narrow direction, small next to everything else in which sentences differ; PCA's largest directions and the full-space distance are dominated by the rest. Who acts is near zero by this measure too (0.01–0.10, panel i): no method found it in GPT-2 small.

PCA shows what varies most, not what matters: a property that changes the stream only along one direction, like sentiment here, can be invisible in the largest PCs and in overall distances, yet almost perfectly readable by a supervised direction. A direction for a property

Tabs 6–7 looked at how a whole set of sentences is arranged, without asking about one property. To find where a property such as sentiment is stored, we need many sentences that differ in that property. Difference in means (DIM) builds a direction from two groups of sentences and asks whether new sentences fall on the right side of it (Lesson 5, tabs 2 and 4). Two contrasts: one its words give away (sentiment), and one no single word gives away (who acts, in active and passive sentences). GPT-2 small, residual stream at the last word of each sentence (the word before the final ".").

1 · The direction

At layer l, average the residual-stream vectors of each group and subtract:

f = mean(x | group A) − mean(x | group B)

What all sentences share (the large common direction that keeps raw cosines high, tab 5) is in both means and cancels, so f keeps only what separates the groups. A new sentence's score is its projection on f: x · f / ‖f‖.

2 · Held out, rank-based

Each sentence is scored on a direction built without it (and without the other sentences made from the same words), so the test is on new sentences. Separation is the selectivity index SI = 2·AUC − 1, where AUC is the fraction of (A, B) pairs in which the A sentence scores higher: 0 = chance, +1 = every A above every B. Only the order of the scores counts, not their level.

A learned direction

Difference in means (tab 8) takes the line between the two group means. A linear probe learns the line instead: it is trained to put one group on one side and the other group on the other. Same model, sentences, position and held-out design as tab 8: GPT-2 small, sentiment and passive role (who acts), residual stream at the last word.

1 · The probe

Logistic regression on the residual stream at layer l:

P(group A | x) = sigmoid(w · x + b)

w and b are fitted to the training sentences, with a penalty λ‖w‖² that keeps w small. A new sentence's score is its projection on w, x · w / ‖w‖, exactly as on f in tab 8, and separation is again SI = 2·AUC − 1.

2 · Probe vs difference in means

DIM uses f = mean(x | A) − mean(x | B). A probe comes closer to Σ−1f, where Σ is the spread of the vectors within each group: it turns the line away from directions in which sentences of the same group differ a lot, since those blur the separation. The penalty is a dial: a large λ pulls w back to f, a small one lets it turn freely. Here λ = 10−5 (each layer's vectors scaled to average length 1).

3 · A probe can fit anything

With 768 dimensions and 20–48 sentences, a probe can separate the training sentences under almost any labelling, even a random one. Two safeguards: every score is held out (the sentence and those made from the same words are left out of the fit), and the whole analysis is repeated with the labels shuffled (20 times). The shuffled runs show what SI a probe reaches when there is nothing to find; a result inside that range is no evidence of a readout.

4 · Sentiment: the probe adds a little

The probe matches or beats difference in means at every layer and reaches 1.00 at layer 10 (panel a: no overlap), far above the shuffled range (up to about 0.5). The gain is largest where the two directions disagree: at layer 0 (0.88 vs 0.76), where the probe's line is turned well away from f (cosine 0.44, panel c), and at layer 11 (0.80 vs 0.60). From layer 3 on the two lines converge (cosine 0.92 at layer 10): once sentiment is a large part of what separates the groups, the line between the means is close to the best line.

5 · Who acts: still nothing

The probe reaches 0.03–0.30, a little above difference in means, but inside the shuffled range at every layer (its 95th percentile is 0.25–0.49; panel e). Its direction stays close to f (cosine 0.84–0.94, panel f): with no separation to improve on, there is little for the probe to correct. So in GPT-2 small, neither method finds a straight-line readout of who acts at the last word.

6 · Four sizes

The four models: small (124M parameters, 12 layers, 768 dimensions), medium (355M, 24, 1,024), large (774M, 36, 1,280) and XL (1.5B, 48, 1,600). In all four the probe reads nothing in the first ~30% of depth, then rises. Small, medium and large differ little: they peak at 0.30, 0.36 and 0.39, late in the network (75–92% of depth), around the range chance reached in small. XL differs in kind: it climbs steeply between 30% and 42% of depth and holds 0.50–0.58 to its last layer, peaking at mid-depth (0.58 at layer 25 of 48). But Fig. 11 has no shuffled-label control for XL, and the score a probe reaches by chance rises with the number of dimensions (1,600 in XL against 768 in small, with the same 48 sentences), so part of XL's lead may be its greater capacity to fit rather than a readout.

2 · Two heads, two steps

Copying "[A][B] … [A] → [B]" takes two heads in different layers. A previous-token head writes into every position which token came just before it, so the position of [B] now also carries "after [A]". Later, at the second [A], an induction head looks for a position marked "after [A]", finds [B], and moves [B] into the prediction. The first head must sit in an earlier layer than the second.

3 · What the two heads do

On the first copy nothing can be predicted (except ":" after "Code", 72%); on the second, the model gives the right word 29–96% (" turtle" after " seven": 80%). Head 4.11 attends to the previous token at every position (1.00 on average; Fig. 13e): the first step. Head 5.5 has nothing to do on the first copy and parks its attention on <|endoftext|>; on the second copy each token attends to the token right after its earlier copy (0.91 on average), the stripe one step off the diagonal (Fig. 13d): the second step. Fig. 13a shows the step from the second " seven": 5.5 puts 89% of its attention on the first " turtle" yet adds only +0.30 directly to the " turtle" logit (17th of the 144 heads). The heads with the largest direct effect sit in layers 9–11 and also look at " turtle" (10.0: +1.79), and two of them push it down (11.10, 10.7). So the induction head finds the token to copy; much of the writing into the prediction is done by later heads (DLA: tab 4).

4 · Inside one head

Fig. 13a and the attention patterns (Fig. 13d, e) show where heads look. To see how a head decides and what it brings back, run_attention_head recomputes one head at one token by hand, step by step, from the weights, and checks every step against the model's own run. Here: head 5.5 at the second " seven" of card 1's sentence (the outlined row of Fig. 13d), in the steps of attention (Lesson 2, tab 3), numbered as in the script and the figure:

  • Steps 1–2 · Query and keys. The head reads the stream entering layer 5 (layer-normed). Its query matrix WQ turns the current token's stream into a 64-number query, "what am I looking for"; its key matrix WK turns every earlier token's stream into a key, "what do I offer".
  • Step 3 · Scores. The query's dot product with each key, divided by √64 = 8 (Fig. 13b). " turtle" scores highest (4.31), <eot> (<|endoftext|>) next (1.87), the rest below 0.5.
  • Step 4 · Softmax. The scores become attention weights, all positive and summing to 1 (Fig. 13c). Done for every query at once, this gives the head's full attention pattern (Fig. 13d), of which the second " seven" is the outlined row. Exponentiating turns a lead of 2.4 into a factor of 11: 0.89 on " turtle", 0.08 on <eot>.
  • Steps 5–6 · Values and output. Each position's value (WV) is what it passes on if attended to; the head adds them up in proportion to the weights and writes the result into the stream through WO.
  • Step 7 · Direct effect on the answer. The head's output, read along the target token's unembedding direction (DLA, tab 4), split by the position it came from (Fig. 13f, g).
  • Step 8 · Which earlier head it depends on. An earlier head is silenced and the attention recomputed (card 5; not in the figure).
5 · Where it looks, and why

Head 5.5 puts 89% of its attention on the first " turtle", the token after the earlier " seven": its query at " seven" matches the key at " turtle", which carries "the token before me was seven" (K-composition: a later head's keys read what an earlier head wrote; Elhage et al. 2021). That information was written there by earlier heads: silencing the previous-token head 4.11 lowers the weight on " turtle" to 73% and moves the rest to <eot> (run_attention_head, step 8: head 4.11's output set to zero). So 4.11 supplies part of the match, not all of it; other earlier heads also write the previous token (card 7: weaker previous-token heads in layers 2 and 3).

6 · What it brings back

Almost all of the head's direct effect on " turtle" comes from the position it attends to (Fig. 13g): 0.288 of the total +0.295. But the value at " turtle" is not specific to turtle (Fig. 13f): the values at " elbow", " spoon" and the second " seven" would push " turtle" just as much (0.29–0.34). So head 5.5 finds the right position, and its own write pushes the answer only weakly and generically; the later heads in layers 9–11 do most of the writing (Fig. 13a). What 5.5 carries may still be read by those later heads rather than by the output directly, which DLA cannot see. Cards 5 and 6 are the two halves of every attention head: where it looks is set by its query and key matrices (the QK circuit), what it brings back by its value and output matrices (the OV circuit).

7 · The same heads every time

On random tokens the effect is extreme: 12.9 nats per token on the first copy, 0.44 on the second (Fig. 14a). Five heads in layers 5–7 do the matching: 5.5 (0.94), 6.9 (0.92), 5.1 (0.91), 7.10 (0.91) and 7.2 (0.84); no other head reaches 0.55 (Fig. 14b). The clearest previous-token head is 4.11 (0.99), one layer below the first induction heads, as the circuit requires; a few weaker ones sit in layers 2, 3 and 6 (0.4–0.55, Fig. 14c).

Remove a part, see what breaks

Tabs 2–10 mostly read the model without changing it (tab 10's step 8 already silenced one head). The tabs in this row intervene: they change something inside the forward pass and watch the output. Ablation is the simplest intervention: remove one component and see what breaks (Lesson 7, tabs 1, 3, 4, 5). Two examples in GPT-2 small: the France prompt of tabs 2–5, where removing parts does surprisingly little, and copying a repeated sequence, where removing three of the induction heads of tab 10 breaks it.

1 · Removing a component

Zero ablation sets the component's output to 0. Mean ablation replaces it with its average output over the prompt's positions (leaving out <|endoftext|>), so the component keeps its typical output and loses only what is specific to each token. Zero can push the model far from anything it saw in training: zeroing attention layer 0 drops the probabilities of both " Paris" and " Marse" to about 0. We use mean ablation, at every position.

2 · Total vs direct effect

The total effect of an ablation is what happens when the model is rerun without the component: everything later layers do differently is included. The direct effect is only the change in what the component itself writes into the last position, read through the final layer norm onto E[" Paris"] − E[" Marse"], as in tab 4. Where the two differ, later layers matter: they use the component's output (an indirect effect) or make up for its loss (self-repair).

3 · France: one essential layer, many small ones

Only MLP 0 is essential: without it " Paris" falls to rank 10 and the model stops answering the question (top token " of"). Its logit difference with " Marse" even rises (+3.97, panel b), because " Marse" falls further. It has no direct effect at all, so everything it does passes through later layers: it acts like a second embedding. Removing MLP 5, 7, 8 or 9 lets " Marse" overtake " Paris" (rank 2; after MLP 3 it is " the"), but the changes are small: " Paris" leads by only 0.09 to start with. No attention layer is needed on its own: " Paris" stays first after every one.

4 · Direct effect is not the whole story

For MLP 10 the two agree (total +0.68, direct +0.79): it pushes " Marse" straight into the output, and removing it helps " Paris". MLP 9 disagrees in sign: its own write favours " Marse" (direct +0.31 when removed), yet removing it hurts " Paris" (total −0.18), so it helps " Paris" through later layers. Attention layers act mostly indirectly: removing layer 2, 4 or 6 helps " Paris" by 0.31–0.45, three to five times their direct effects; for layer 0 the direct effect even has the other sign (−0.08, total +0.54); only for layer 9 are the two close (0.27 direct, 0.38 total). Tab 4's direct logit attribution sees none of this.

5 · Copying: a few heads do it

GPT-2 small predicts the second copy of a random sequence almost perfectly (0.44 nats per token, against 12.9 for the first copy; tab 10). Ablating its five induction heads, 5.5, 6.9, 5.1, 7.10 and 7.2, raises the second-copy loss to 5.8 nats; ablating five random heads changes nothing (0.47).

6 · With some backup

Removing one or two of them barely matters (0.50, 0.60): the others cover. The third does it (3.08). Even with the eight best removed (the five above plus 10.1, 9.6 and 9.9, scores about 0.5), the second copy stays easier than the first (7.1 vs 12.9 nats): heads with weaker copying scores remain, such as 10.7 and 5.0 (0.48), and can still do part of the job. So copying is localized but redundant.

When ablation is informative: when a function lives in a few components, as copying does, removing them breaks it and nothing else matters as much. When it is spread out and backed up, as the France fact is, single removals do little and the effects are mostly indirect. For example, removing heads 10.0 and 9.8 on "The capital of Greece is the city of" fails for that reason: both " Athens" and " Pir" fall, " Athens" stays first, and heads in layer 11 take over.

A circuit: how GPT-2 finds the indirect object

Tabs 4 and 10–12 asked which single component matters. A circuit is the next step: several components, each with a job, passing information in order. The best-studied circuit in GPT-2 small does indirect object identification (IOI; Wang et al. 2022, Lesson 7, tab 5): in "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to", the answer is " Mary", the name that appears once (the indirect object, IO), not " John", the name that appears twice (the subject, S). Three tools from earlier tabs find its main parts: direct logit attribution per head (tab 4), attention patterns (tab 10), and patching one head at one position (tab 12).

1 · The task and the measurements

Positions (0-based; the labels of Wang et al.):

  • IO = the indirect object, " Mary" (position 2). It appears once, and it is the right answer.
  • S1 = the first mention of the subject, " John" (position 4), in "When Mary and John went…".
  • S2 = the second mention of the same subject, " John" (position 10), in "…the store, John gave a drink to".
  • END = the last token, " to" (position 14), where the model predicts the answer.

Metric: LD = logit(" Mary") − logit(" John") at END: +3.36 for the clean prompt. Corrupted prompt: S2 replaced by " Mary" ("…, Mary gave a drink to"): now Mary is the repeated name and the model prefers " John" (LD −2.48). Three measurements: where the heads look from END (Fig. 19); each head's direct contribution to LD at END (Fig. 20a); and denoising one head at one position: run the corrupted prompt, copy in that head's output from the clean run at END (Fig. 20b) or at S2 (Fig. 20c), and score (LDpatched − LDcorrupt) / (LDclean − LDcorrupt), 1 = that head alone restores the clean answer.

2 · Name movers write the answer

The name movers 9.6 and 9.9 each add +2.1 to LD, 10.0 +0.8 (Figs. 20a and 19). From END they look at " Mary" (0.67, 0.67, 0.33 of their attention; at most 0.11 on either " John") and copy the name they see into the output. Negative name movers do the opposite: 10.7 also looks at " Mary" (0.82) but writes −2.3 against it, 11.10 −0.8, so the model hedges its answer (Wang et al. found the same heads).

3 · S-inhibition heads: which name not to copy

The name movers look at " Mary" and not at " John", but nothing in a name mover's own input at END says which name was repeated. That information comes from four heads in layers 7 and 8, 7.3, 7.9, 8.6 and 8.10, which Wang et al. called S-inhibition heads (S for subject: they inhibit the name movers' attention to the subject). Where they look: from END, at S2, the repeated " John" (Fig. 19: 8.6 79%, 8.10 46%, 7.9 41%, 7.3 13%), and almost not at all at " Mary". What they read there: by layer 7 the stream at S2 carries the mark "this name appeared before", written by the heads of card 4; the S-inhibition heads copy it to END. What they do with it: they do not write a name themselves (their direct contributions are small, +0.05 to +0.41, the numbers in brackets in Fig. 19). Instead their output changes the queries of the name movers in layers 9–10, so those queries match " John" less and " Mary" more. One head's output shaping a later head's query is called Q-composition, the query-side counterpart of the K-composition between the previous-token and induction heads of tab 10. Wang et al. found that the signal they pass on is partly the name itself and mostly its position.

The evidence. The name is confusing: the S-inhibition heads themselves attend to the subject (Fig. 19); what they inhibit is the name movers' attention to it. Fig. 21 tests this directly, with the name movers' attention from END to each name in four runs (a–c) and the answer (d). In the clean run they look at " Mary" (0.67, 0.67, 0.33) and hardly at " John". In the corrupted run, where " Mary" is the repeated name, they flip and look at " John" (9.9: 0.84), and the model answers " John" (LD −2.48). Copying in only the four S-inhibition heads' clean output at END flips them back (0.69, 0.71, 0.33 on " Mary"), although everything else in the run still says " Mary" is repeated, and LD returns to +3.18. Mean-ablating the four heads at END in the clean run does the opposite: the name movers split their attention between the two names (9.9: 0.27 on " Mary", 0.35 on " John") and LD falls from +3.36 to +1.40. So the decision "avoid the repeated name" reaches the name movers through these four heads. One at a time they matter less (Fig. 20b: 8.10 0.24, 8.6 0.21, 7.9 0.17, 7.3 0.14, despite their small direct effects); together they restore 0.97, more than the sum of their single scores (0.76).

4 · Where the "repeated" mark comes from

The S-inhibition heads of card 3 read a mark at S2 that says "this name appeared before". Fig. 20c finds the heads that write it: one head at a time, its clean output copied in at S2 in the corrupted run. These heads do not stand out in Figs. 19 and 20a, which look only at END: they work at S2, and only indirectly (what they write at S2 is read by the S-inhibition heads, not turned into the answer). Fig. 22 shows where they look from S2. Two kinds of head, both in layers 0–6:

  • Duplicate-token heads (3.0, 0.1). At the second " John" they look back for an earlier copy of the same token and find the first " John" (Fig. 22; 3.0: 71% of its attention, 0.1: 52%). What they write there amounts to "this token has occurred before". 3.0 alone restores 0.10.
  • Induction heads (5.5, one of the induction heads of tabs 10–11). At the second " John", 5.5 looks at the token right after the first " John", " went" (92%): the same move it makes in a repeated sequence, where it predicts what came next last time. Here what matters is that it found an earlier " John" at all: another sign that this name is a repeat. 5.5 alone restores 0.18, the most of any head at S2. 6.9 (0.08) and 5.9 (0.06) add a little; their attention from S2 is less specific (6.9 looks mostly at <|endoftext|>, 5.9 is spread out), so their role here is less clear.

Copied in together, these five heads (0.1, 3.0, 5.5, 5.9, 6.9) restore 0.56 of the answer. This is where the circuit starts: without the mark, the S-inhibition heads would have nothing to carry.

5 · The whole circuit
  1. At S2, layers 0–6: duplicate-token and induction heads mark the second " John" as a repeat (card 4; Figs. 20c, 22).
  2. At END, layers 7–8: S-inhibition heads read that mark and carry it to the last token (card 3; Figs. 19, 21).
  3. At END, layers 9–10: name movers look at the name that is not marked, " Mary", and copy it into the answer (card 2; Figs. 19, 20a).
  4. At END, layers 10–11: negative name movers (10.7, 11.10) also look at " Mary" but write against it, so the answer is hedged (card 2).

Nothing in this is specific to Mary and John: "copy the name that was not repeated" works for any pair. The same heads come out for 8 other name pairs in the same sentence (Alice/Bob, Sarah/Tom, … Rachel/Michael): in every one the top three name movers are 9.6, 9.9 and 10.0 (once 10.10 in place of 10.0), the negative ones 10.7 and 11.10, the top heads at END include 8.6, 8.10 and 7.9, and at S2 5.5 comes first.

Write along a direction, see what the model says

Tab 8 found a sentiment direction that a linear readout can use; reading a property does not show that the model uses it. Steering tests that by writing: add the direction to the residual stream while the model runs, and see whether its output becomes more positive or negative (Lesson 7, tab 6). GPT-2 small, the 10 sentiment pairs of tab 8, and the prompt "I think this movie is".

1 · The method

Direction: f = mean(x | positive sentence) − mean(x | negative sentence), the difference in means of tab 8, here with each sentence's vector the mean over its tokens, at one point of the residual stream. Steering: run the test prompt with α · f added to that point at every position (except <|endoftext|>), so every later layer reads the shifted stream. α = 1 adds the full difference between the two groups' means; α < 0 pushes toward negative. f is small: at the output of layer 5 its length is 0.13 of the stream's typical norm, so α = 4 adds about half the stream's norm. Readout: the next-token sentiment score, the mean logit of 5 positive words (" great", " good", " wonderful", " excellent", " amazing") minus 5 negative ones (" terrible", " bad", " awful", " horrible", " boring"); and a 20-token continuation, choosing the most likely word each time (greedy). Control: 10 shuffled copies of f: its own values in a random order, so the same length and the same values but a random direction (almost perpendicular to f).

2 · What the push does to the residual stream

Each layer adds its attention and MLP writes to the stream; the push is one extra term, added once (here at the output of layer 5) and then carried to the end.

Without steering
x(0) = x(emb) + Δxattn,0 + Δxmlp,0
…
x(5) = x(4) + Δxattn,5 + Δxmlp,5
x(6) = x(5) + Δxattn,6 + Δxmlp,6
…
x(11) = x(emb) + Σl = 0…11 (Δxattn,l + Δxmlp,l)
With α · f added at the output of layer 5
x(0) … x(5): unchanged
x̃(5) = x(5) + α f  (the push, not a layer's write)
x̃(6) = x̃(5) + Δx̃attn,6 + Δx̃mlp,6
…
x̃(11) = x(emb) + Σl = 0…5 (Δxattn,l + Δxmlp,l) + α f + Σl = 6…11 (Δx̃attn,l + Δx̃mlp,l)

Layers before the push (0–5) write exactly what they wrote before. The push stays in the stream: each later layer only adds to the stream, so α f is still in x̃(11) and reaches the logits directly through the unembedding (the direct path of tab 4). Later writes change (the tilde): layers 6–11 read x + α f instead of x, so their attention patterns and MLP outputs shift; the push is added at every position, so attention at the last token also reads pushed vectors from the earlier tokens. This is the indirect path, the model's own response to the push. The change in the logits is the sum of the two. Because each layer reads its input through LayerNorm, what counts is the push's size relative to the stream (0.13 · α of its norm at layer 5). During generation the same push is added at every forward pass, so each new token is steered too.

Rewrite one fact in the weights

Tab 12 found where a fact is read: middle-layer MLPs at the subject (Fig. 18). Tab 14 changed what the model says by pushing its activations, for one run only. ROME (Rank-One Model Editing; Meng, Bau, Andonian & Belinkov 2022, "Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT", NeurIPS; Lesson 7, tab 7) rewrites a fact in the weights themselves: one rank-one change to one MLP's output matrix, meant to make the model give the new fact in every context. How close it comes is what this tab measures. GPT-2 XL, as in the paper (in GPT-2 small the edit barely carries over to other wordings), and the fact of tab 14, card 4, with the edit prompt "The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of" → " Rome" instead of " Paris".

1 · An MLP as a key–value memory

At the subject's last token (" Tower"), an MLP's hidden activation k (after GELU: the vector that Wout multiplies) can be read as a key, and what the MLP writes into the residual stream, v = Wout k + bout, as the value stored under it. Later layers turn that value into the answer. To change the fact, change what Wout returns for this one key, and as little as possible for every other key. The smallest change that does this is rank one: a single outer product (a column times a row) added to Wout.

2 · The edit in four steps

Notation. k: any key (the MLP's hidden activation at any token); k*: the subject's key, averaged over many contexts (step 1); kE: the key at the edit prompt itself, with no prefix. The star follows the paper, which writes the inserted pair as (k*, v*): the key to edit and the new value it should return (here v* = old value + δ).

  1. The key k*. The edit prompt is run behind 20 short texts that GPT-2 XL writes itself (ten of 5 tokens and ten of 10, such as "You can't have a good game without a good soundtrack. "), and once without a prefix: 21 prompts. Each gives one vector: layer 17's MLP hidden activation (after GELU; 6,400 numbers) at the subject's last token, " Tower" (the subject is four tokens, " E", "iff", "el", " Tower"; Meng et al.'s causal tracing finds facts read at its last one; in Fig. 18 the last piece of "Eiffel", "el", matters even more). k* is the average of these 21 vectors, so the edit does not depend on the exact wording before the subject. Only the text before " Tower" changes its key (attention is causal), which is why the prompts differ in their prefixes. The paper's Section 3.1 describes averaging k* over 50 generated texts of 2 to 10 tokens; its Appendix E.5 and its released code, which we follow, use 20 (ten of 5 tokens and ten of 10) plus the prompt alone: the same 21 prompts in steps 1 and 2.
  2. The new value. The old value v0 is what layer 17's MLP writes at " Tower" in the edit prompt now (1,600 numbers). The new value is v* = v0 + δ, where δ (also 1,600 numbers) starts at zero and is found by gradient descent: add δ to the MLP's output at " Tower", run layers 18–47 to the end, and measure the loss
    Loss(δ) = −(1/21) Σj log p(" Rome" | promptj, δ) + 0.0625 · KL(pδ ‖ pbefore) + 0.5 · |δ| / |v0|²
    First term: make the model say " Rome": p(" Rome" | promptj, δ) is the probability that the next token is " Rome" after each of the 21 prompts ("…The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of"), with δ added.
    Second term: keep what the tower is. It uses a second prompt, the essence prompt "The Eiffel Tower is a", which names the subject without asking where it is. pbefore and pδ are the model's whole next-token distributions after it (50,257 probabilities each), without and with δ. KL is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, Σw pδ(w) log(pδ(w) / pbefore(w)), with the edited distribution first, as in the paper: 0 if δ leaves the distribution unchanged, larger the more δ makes words likely that were not likely before. Without it, δ could reach " Rome" by changing what the model thinks the tower is (the paper calls this essence drift).
    Third term: keep δ small, relative to the old value v0. This form, |δ| rather than |δ|², is the paper's released code; it is a weak penalty, so in practice δ is limited mainly by a cap in the same released code (clamp_norm_factor = 4), |δ| ≤ 4 |v0|.
    Backpropagation through the frozen layers 18–47 gives the gradient of the loss with respect to δ, and Adam updates δ; 20 steps. No weight changes in this step: like training, but δ is the only thing being trained. In our run p(" Rome") reached 0.99 by step 8, and δ grew until it hit the cap (4 |v0|) at step 9; the remaining steps reshaped δ at that length, bringing the essence penalty down from its peak of 2.0 (step 7) to 0.45 (Fig. 24c).
  3. The key covariance C = the mean of k k⊤ over 100,000 tokens of Wikipedia text: which key directions ordinary text uses. (A tiny ridge is added, so C can be inverted where some directions never occur.)
  4. The update ΔW = δ u⊤ / (u⊤ kE), with u = C−1k*. Its column δ is the change to the output; its row u makes it respond along the subject's key but away from the key directions that other text uses a lot; the scale makes it exact at the edit prompt's own key kE (ΔW kE = δ; the paper's equation uses k* here, its released code kE). For any token, the edited MLP then writes (Wout + ΔW) k = Wout k + δ · (u⊤ k) / (u⊤ kE): its old output plus δ, scaled by how much that token's key resembles the subject's. That is exactly δ at kE, and close to nothing for keys from unrelated text. In our run |ΔW| is 7.4% of |Wout|.

How the steps fit together. Steps 1 and 2 are independent: step 1 finds which address to change (k*), step 2 what to store there (δ). Step 2 does not use k*: it adds δ to the MLP's output at " Tower" in each prompt, whatever key that prompt produces, and uses only the old value v0 at the edit prompt (from kE) to set the scale of its size penalty. Step 4 combines them, with C from step 3: k* and C give the edit's row (u = C−1k*), δ its column, and kE its scale.

3 · What the edit does

The edit works, and it is specific (panels a, b). 4 · Limitations
  • Generalization depends on the setup. Here all four paraphrases flip with the paper's 20 generated prefixes, less strongly with 5 fixed prefixes, and at layers 8, 12 and 32–44 only one to three of the four do (Fig. 25). The paper's benchmark reports averages over many facts (about 96% of paraphrases flip); a single fact can do much worse.
  • Consequences follow only partly. Here the tower moved to Italy as well as to Rome, but the reverse direction ("the most famous tower in Rome is…") keeps the old answer, the reversal curse of Lesson 6, tab 4. Cohen et al. (2024, "Evaluating the Ripple Effects of Knowledge Editing") found this for ROME and other editors.
  • Specificity is only tested where we look. Four neighbours are checked; an edit can change facts nobody tests, and there is no general way to verify that nothing else broke.
  • Where a fact is found is not where it is best edited. Hase et al. (2023, "Does Localization Inform Editing?") found that causal tracing barely predicts which layer gives the best edit. Fig. 25 compares the two for this fact.
  • Edits do not stack well. One edit is small; many sequential edits gradually damage the model and undo earlier edits (Gupta et al. 2024). MEMIT (Meng et al. 2023) spreads many edits over several layers instead.
  • It assumes one memory. ROME treats one MLP layer as the store of the fact; tab 12 found facts read by many middle-layer MLPs together, so a single-layer edit changes only one of several contributions.

Rewrite the stream as a few readable features

The residual stream of GPT-2 small has 768 numbers per token, but the model represents far more concepts than that: they share the 768 dimensions as overlapping directions (superposition; Lesson 7, tab 8). A sparse autoencoder (SAE) learns a much larger set of directions, here 24,576, chosen so that each token's stream is rebuilt from only a few of them. If it works, each direction, a feature, stands for one recognizable thing, and the stream can be read as a short list of what is active. The method was introduced for language models by Cunningham et al. (2023) and Bricken et al. (2023, "Towards Monosemanticity"), and scaled up by Templeton et al. (2024, "Scaling Monosemanticity"). This tab trains one on GPT-2 small, with the recipe Anthropic published in April 2024 (Conerly et al., used for Scaling Monosemanticity), and follows it step by step: the data, the loss, training, how good the result is, what single features mean, and what they do when turned off or up.

1 · The idea

An SAE has two halves (Fig. 26). The encoder maps a token's stream x (768 numbers) to a vector f of 24,576 feature activations, which say how strongly each feature is present; a ReLU sets the negative values to exactly 0, so most of them are 0. The decoder rebuilds the stream from them: each feature i has a decoder direction di (column i of Wdec, 768 numbers), learned in training and fixed afterwards, and the reconstruction is x̂ = (Σi fi di + bdec) / c = (Wdec f + bdec) / c: the active features' directions, each scaled by how strongly it fires, plus a bias shared by all tokens. c is one fixed number that scales every stream to the same average length before it enters the SAE (card 2); dividing by it returns x̂ to the stream's own units. fi says whether and how much a feature is present, and is computed anew for every token; di says how it is written into the stream, and is the same for every token. The SAE is trained only to rebuild x from few active features; nobody tells it what the features should mean. Sizes: x and x̂ are 768 × 1 (the stream's width); f is 24,576 × 1 (one number per feature); each decoder direction di is 768 × 1, a direction in the stream. Vectors are columns here, as in run_sae (Lesson 7 writes the same with rows and calls the activations z). f follows the SAE papers here: it is not the f of tabs 8, 9 and 14, where f is the difference-in-means direction, a 768 × 1 direction in the stream like di.

2 · The data: GPT-2's own stream, 820 million tokens

One training example is one token's stream leaving layer 5: a single vector of 768 numbers (the x of card 1), not a sequence. Call it a token vector. It already carries what GPT-2 computed from the tokens before it, so " of" after "the city" gives a different vector from " of" after "a cup". Layer 5 is the sixth of twelve, the middle of the model (Templeton et al. also used a middle layer). The France prompt gives 8 token vectors, one per token after the start token <|endoftext|>.

How many: 820 million token vectors, one from every token of 820 million tokens of OpenWebText, web text of the kind GPT-2 was trained on. Each training step uses 4,096 token vectors; 200,000 steps use each vector once.

How they are made: GPT-2 reads text 1,024 tokens at a time (its context length), so the text is cut into chunks of 1,024 tokens, and each chunk yields 1,023 token vectors (the first is left out, below). These go into a pool of about 2 million token vectors from about 2,000 chunks, shuffled. Each step takes its 4,096 token vectors from the pool, about as many as 4 chunks hold but drawn from many chunks, so that a step is not dominated by a few stretches of consecutive text; when half the pool is used, GPT-2 refills it with 1,024 new chunks. The two numbers are independent: 1,024 is fixed by GPT-2, 4,096 is the recipe's batch size. Nothing is stored on disk (820 million vectors of 768 numbers would fill about 1.3 TB).

Three details:

  • The first token of each chunk is left out. GPT-2 parks attention there (the "attention sink"), and its stream is many times longer than any other token's; a few such token vectors would dominate the loss.
  • One scale for all. Every token vector is multiplied by the same number, c = 0.354, which makes the average length √768 ≈ 27.7, as the recipe prescribes, so that the same λ means the same in any model or layer.
  • The same token vectors, in the same order, for all three SAEs (λ = 1, 5 and 20), so they differ only in the penalty.

An SAE describes only this one point in the model. Covering every layer takes one SAE per layer, as in Gemma Scope (Lieberum et al. 2024, SAEs on all layers and sublayers of Gemma 2 2B and 9B), or a crosscoder, one sparse dictionary that reads and rebuilds several layers at once (Lindsey et al. 2024).

3 · The SAE and its loss

For one token, with the scaled stream c x:

f = ReLU(Wenc (c x) + benc)   24,576 activations, most exactly 0
x̂ = (Wdec f + bdec) / c   the reconstruction, 768 numbers
Loss = ‖c x − (Wdec f + bdec)‖² + λ Σi fi ‖di‖   the loss of each token vector, averaged over the 4,096 of a step

What is learned. Wenc, benc, Wdec and bdec (table below) are the SAE's parameters: none is set by hand, all are found by gradient descent on this loss (card 4). The decoder directions are simply the columns of Wdec: di is column i, so Wdec = [d0 d1 … d24,575], and learning Wdec means learning all 24,576 directions: at every step, each di is nudged toward whatever its feature's tokens still need for their reconstruction. The activations f, by contrast, are not parameters: the encoder computes them from each token vector.

The loss has two parts that pull against each other. The reconstruction error wants x̂ as close to x as possible, which is easiest with many active features. The sparsity penalty charges for every active feature, in proportion to its activation fi times the length of its decoder direction ‖di‖. λ sets the exchange rate: a large λ buys sparsity with accuracy. The penalty is an L1 norm, a stand-in for what we really want, the number of active features (L0), which has no useful gradient.

Why ‖di‖ is in the penalty. What a feature adds to the reconstruction is fi di, its activation times its direction. The same contribution can be made in many ways: activation 10 with a direction of length 1, activation 1 with length 10, or activation 0.1 with length 100. A penalty on the activations alone, Σi fi (a plain L1 penalty), would charge 10, 1 or 0.1 for these, so the optimizer could make the penalty almost vanish by making every activation tiny and every direction long, without switching a single feature off. Charging fi ‖di‖ instead gives 10 in all three cases: it measures the size of the contribution itself, which rescaling cannot change, so the only way to lower it is to use fewer features, or weaker ones. (Bricken et al. closed the same loophole differently, by fixing every direction's length at 1.)

Why 24,576 features. It is 32 × 768, small enough to train in under 3 hours on one GPU. It is not the number of concepts in the model; there is no fixed number. In a larger SAE, features split into finer ones (Bricken et al.; Templeton et al. went to 1, 4 and 34 million features on Claude 3 Sonnet), so a feature here, such as "France" (card 7), may become several, for France in different contexts. No feature here is dead at λ = 5 (card 5), a sign that a larger dictionary would still find use for more.

What happens in one training step. In each step:
  • 4,096 different token vectors go in, each used once in the whole training run;
  • 4,096 activation vectors f come out, one per token vector, each with 24,576 entries;
  • 4,096 reconstructions x̂, one per f;
  • 4,096 losses, one per token vector, each a single number: its reconstruction error plus λ times its sparsity penalty.
Then:
  • the 4,096 losses are averaged into one number;
  • all parameters are updated once from that average (card 4).
The next step takes 4,096 new token vectors and computes their f's with the slightly updated encoder, and so on, 200,000 times. So a given f exists only within its step: it is computed, used for its loss and gradient, then discarded. What training keeps is the parameters; with them, f can be recomputed later for any text (cards 6–9).
4 · Training

Training follows Anthropic's recipe (Conerly et al. 2024) exactly. In order:

  1. Starting values. Each decoder direction di points in a random direction and has length 0.1. The encoder starts as the decoder's transpose, Wenc = Wdec⊤, so each feature starts out responding to the same direction it writes. Both biases start at 0.
  2. How the parameters are updated (once per step, as summarized at the end of card 3). They are updated with Adam, the standard variant of gradient descent that adapts the step size of each parameter (learning rate 5 × 10−5, no weight decay). If the gradient is unusually large it is first scaled down to length 1 (clipping), so that one odd batch cannot throw the parameters off. Averaging the losses of 4,096 tokens from many texts makes each update follow what improves the SAE for typical tokens, not for one.
  3. Two settings change during training (everything else stays constant). λ rises from 0 to its final value over the first 10,000 steps (5%), so the SAE first learns to reconstruct and only then to be sparse. The learning rate falls linearly to 0 over the last 40,000 steps (20%), so the parameters settle instead of jittering around their final values.
  4. Once, after training: every decoder direction is rescaled to length 1. Training leaves each di with its own length, say 2.5. Dividing di by 2.5 gives it length 1. To keep what the feature writes, fi di, unchanged, fi must then be 2.5 times larger for every token. Since fi is computed by the encoder, fi = ReLU((row i of Wenc) · (c x) + benc,i), that means multiplying the feature's row of Wenc and its bias benc,i by 2.5: the number inside the ReLU becomes 2.5 times larger, and a ReLU of a number 2.5 times larger is 2.5 times larger (a negative number stays at 0). Every reconstruction stays exactly the same; only the bookkeeping moves. Now ‖di‖ = 1 for every feature, so an activation alone says how much a feature writes: feature 12 at 13.8 writes 13.8 units along its direction, and can be compared directly with feature 13788 at 3.1.

Each SAE took about 2 hours 50 minutes on one RTX 3080 Ti, including GPT-2's forward passes.

What training does

At the start every feature fires on every token (L0 ≈ 22,500 of 24,576, Fig. 27b): with Wenc = Wdec⊤, each feature responds to whatever it points towards, and the reconstruction is easy (variance explained 0.95–0.99). As λ rises, features switch off. By step 10,000 (λ at its final value) L0 is down to 444 (λ = 1) and 114 (λ = 5), and the reconstruction has paid for it (Fig. 27c). The remaining 190,000 steps are slow refinement: L0 keeps falling (to 147 and 19) while the reconstruction holds or improves slightly, so the SAE finds sparser ways to say the same thing. For λ = 5, the sparsity term ends a little above the reconstruction error (125 against 102 per token, Fig. 27a): the two are balanced, as the recipe intends. The learning-rate decay at the end changes almost nothing here.

5 · How good is it? Sparse against accurate

Five measures. The first three say how well the SAE does its job (how sparse, how accurate, and how much of GPT-2's behaviour it keeps), on 2 million tokens of validation text it never trained on; the last two say whether its 24,576 features are used sensibly, on 10 million evaluation tokens: more training text that the SAE never saw.

1 · L0: the average number of active features per token (fi > 0).

2 · Variance explained by x̂: 1 − Σ ‖x − x̂‖² / Σ ‖x − mean(x)‖², summed over the same tokens. It is the reconstruction error as a fraction of how much the token vectors vary around their mean, mean(x), the average token vector of the sample (1 = perfect; 0 = no better than giving every token the mean vector; the scale c cancels). Equivalently, variance explained = 1 − normalized mean squared error (the reconstruction's squared error divided by that of the mean vector); Conerly et al. report normalized MSE (0.16 for a residual-stream SAE of a 4-layer model at λ = 5, about 0.84 variance explained if normalized the same way) without spelling out what they normalize by.

3 · Loss recovered, the measure that matters most: GPT-2's next-token loss with x̂ spliced in place of the real stream after layer 5, compared with the clean model (3.14 nats per token) and with the stream there set to zero (11.73). The fraction of that gap the SAE closes is the loss recovered: loss recovered = (zero − SAE) / (zero − clean); for λ = 5, (11.73 − 3.80) / (11.73 − 3.14) = 0.92. 1 means the SAE loses nothing; 0 means it is as bad as no stream at all. (This is GPT-2's next-token loss, not the SAE's training loss of card 3.)

4 · Dead features: the percentage of features that never fire in the 10 million tokens, wasted capacity.

What the numbers say

No setting is best at everything. λ = 1 rebuilds the stream well (99% of the loss recovered) but uses about 150 features per token, and 17.6% of its features fire on more than 1% of all tokens: too common to stand for anything specific. λ = 20 is very sparse (2.8 features per token) but loses 3.28 nats per token. λ = 5, the recipe's default, sits between: 19 features per token, 80% of the variance, 92% of the loss recovered, and almost no dense features. It meets the bar Templeton et al. set for their SAEs (L0 below 300, at least 65% variance explained), and it is the SAE used for the rest of the tab. Almost no feature is dead: none at λ = 1 and 5, 0.03% (7 features) at λ = 20 (Templeton et al. had 2% dead with 1 million features, 65% with 34 million): 24,576 features is small for this recipe. One limitation: the SAE explains 80% of the variance, so a fifth of what the stream holds is in the error term, which this tab does not interpret.

Nothing in training named the features, so their meaning has to be read off afterwards. Two standard readings:

  1. Where it fires most: the tokens with the highest activation in the 10 million evaluation tokens (card 5), with their context (what the feature responds to).
  2. What it writes: its decoder direction passed straight through the unembedding, WU di, as in the logit lens (tab 3). The tokens with the largest values are the ones the feature pushes the next-token prediction towards; this ignores everything the later layers do with it (what the feature says).

8 · What a feature does: turn it off, or up

The SAE splits each token's stream into pieces: the features' contributions, the bias, and what the SAE misses (the error, x − x̂):

x = Σi fi di / c + bdec / c + error

To set feature i to a value v, change only its term and keep the rest, the error included:

x′ = x + (v − fi) di / c

The bias and the error do not appear because they are already inside x and stay as they are. Written out, the edit replaces a single term:

x′ = f1 d1 / c + f2 d2 / c + … + v di / c + … + bdec / c + error   ← only feature i's term changed

The short form is what the code uses: it needs only feature i's activation and direction. The edit is applied to the stream after layer 5 at every position except the first. v = 0 turns the feature off (its contribution fi di / c is subtracted; where it did not fire, nothing changes); a large v clamps it on (Templeton et al.'s feature steering, the SAE version of tab 14's steering with a direction).

In short: clamping sets feature i's contribution to the layer-5 stream to v di / c at every position (except the first), and leaves the other features' contributions to that stream as they were; the later layers, and every token generated after, then respond to the changed stream.

Clamped gently, each feature bends the text toward its meaning while keeping it fluent; clamped harder, it takes over. In our tests (six features, two prompts; the table shows a selection), 0.5 × the maximum usually bent the text while keeping it fluent, 1 × broke some features into repetition ("capital", 20664) but not others ("terrible"), and 2 × or more broke the one feature tried (20664: 2, 5 and 10 × all give "K K K…"). Templeton et al. clamped features in Claude 3 Sonnet to 5 × their maximum and more; a small model edited at every position tolerates much less.

Negative clamps are allowed too (Templeton et al. use them): they write the feature's direction backwards, into a stream the encoder never produces, since its activations cannot be negative. Here they mostly weakened the concept rather than producing its opposite: at −0.25 × "wonderful" the walk loses its "very nice and kind" people and turns neutral, and at −1 × "France" the food stays chicken, now a curry. Even −1 × the maximum kept the text fluent, for the three features tried (two are shown in the table).

9 · Features against the directions of tabs 8 and 14

Tab 8 found one direction that separates positive from negative sentences: the difference of the mean streams at layer 5, on 20 sentences (the direction tab 14 steered with). How do the SAE's features relate to it? Most decoder directions are nearly perpendicular to it (Fig. 32a); only 33 of 24,576 have a cosine above 0.3 in size. Two are clear-cut: feature 265 (cosine +0.31), whose top contexts are "wonderful", and feature 20419 (−0.38), whose top contexts are "terrible" and "awful".

On the 20 sentences themselves (Fig. 32b), feature 265 fires in 3 of the 10 positive sentences and 0 of the negative ones; feature 20419 in 4 of the 10 negative sentences and 0 of the positive ones. It is a word family, not one word: 265 fires in the sentences with "wonderful", "fantastic" and "brilliant"; 20419 in those with "terrible", "awful", "horrible" and "miserable". Each fires only where its kind of word occurs, and is silent in the other sentences. The sentiment direction is an average over all sentences; the SAE instead has many specific features that each carry part of it. Both views are useful: the direction is one handle for the whole concept; the features say which words and contexts make it up.

A factual concept: France → Italy. Tab 14 also steered with a direction from France to Italy, built the same way from 10 matched pairs ("I spent the summer in Italy." / "… in France."). Here the picture is much simpler (Fig. 32c, d). Only 6 features have a cosine above 0.3 in size, and the two largest are the two countries: feature 17482, whose top context is "Italy" (cosine +0.56), and feature 12, "France" (−0.40), the feature of card 7. On the 20 sentences, 17482 fires in all 10 Italy sentences and none of the France ones, and feature 12 in all 10 France sentences and none of the Italy ones. So the France → Italy direction is close to "the Italy feature minus the France feature": a concept carried by one word in every sentence maps onto single features, while sentiment, carried by many different words, is spread over many.

To reproduce

There are two stages: training the SAE in Python, then analysing it in MATLAB.

1. Train (Python, GPU, hours). train_sae.py trains one SAE in PyTorch, which takes about 2 hours 50 minutes on one RTX 3080 Ti (12 GB). It reads three inputs:

  • OpenWebText tokenized with GPT-2's tokenizer, in train.bin and val.bin.
  • GPT-2 small's weights, in gpt2_full_weights.mat. The script runs GPT-2 itself to get the stream after layer 5.
  • A JSON file per SAE, listing only the settings that differ from the defaults.

For example:

python "Python code/train_sae.py" "Python code/sae_l5_lam5.json"

% sae_l5_lam5.json:
{"out_dir": "runs/sae_l5_lam5", "lam": 5.0}

% the defaults:
layer      = 5          % the stream leaving this layer (0-based)
d_sae      = 24576      % features
lam        = 5.0        % sparsity penalty λ (1, 5, 20 here)
lr         = 5e-5       % Adam, no weight decay; linear decay to 0 over the last 20% (lr_decay = 0.2)
steps      = 200000;  batch = 4096       % 820 million tokens, each used once
lam_warmup = 0.05       % λ rises from 0 over the first 5% of steps
init_norm  = 0.1;  grad_clip = 1.0;  buffer_windows = 2048;  seed = 1

It saves two files in out_dir:

  • sae.mat, the trained SAE: Wenc, benc, Wdec, bdec, c and the layer. The decoder directions are already rescaled to length 1.
  • eval.mat, the evaluation on the 10 million evaluation tokens: each feature's firing count, its mean activation, and its 20 top activations with their positions in train.bin. The scores of card 5 are also saved in eval.json.

2. Analyse (MATLAB, CPU, minutes). run_sae trains nothing. It loads sae.mat and eval.mat from run_dir, loads GPT-2 small's weights to run the prompts, and reads train.bin only to print the text around each top activation. Its settings are:

run_dir      = fullfile(project_root(), 'runs', 'sae_l5_lam5');   % train_sae.py's output folder
prompt       = 'The capital of France is the city of';
target       = ' Paris';   rival = ' London';
feature      = [];          % [] = the most active feature at the last token (20664 here); 12 for France
steer_prompt = 'I went for a walk and saw';
clamps       = [0 0.25 0.5 1];   % multiples of the feature's largest activation

It prints each step of this tab for one feature (its top contexts, the tokens it promotes, the splice test, the comparison with the sentiment direction, and the steered continuations) and plots the feature on the prompt, the feature density and its promoted tokens. It runs in a few minutes on a CPU.
