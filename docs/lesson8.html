Lesson 8

LLMs as Neural Systems to Study: The Electrophysiology Angle

What does it look like to study a language model the way a neuroscientist studies a brain? From the fundamental analogy to the geometry of residual stream dynamics.

The Fundamental Analogy

Neuroscience spent much of the 20th century trying to understand how populations of neurons represent and transform information. The core empirical tool was the electrode: place a probe near neurons, record their voltage spikes, and try to infer what the population is "computing." Decades of progress followed — from simple receptive fields in V1 to population vectors in motor cortex to place cells in hippocampus.

LLMs offer something stranger and more tractable: a neural network with the same functional challenge (mapping inputs to outputs via learned representations), but with full observability. We can record from every "neuron" at every "timestep." We can replay stimuli exactly. We can intervene with surgical precision. We can examine the weights that produced the behavior. In many ways, an LLM is the dream experiment that biological neuroscience never gets to run.

The core analogy: Tokens in an LLM correspond roughly to stimuli. Layers correspond to time (or to the depth of processing). The d dimensions of the residual stream are most analogous to neurons. The residual stream at each layer is the population state — a high-dimensional vector summarizing "what the network knows" at that moment. Studying how this state evolves from layer to layer is, structurally, very similar to studying how neural population activity evolves over time during a task. For autoregressive generation, each forward pass is analogous to a trial dependent on the previous trial history. While these analogies are imperfect, we will explore if any concepts from electrophysiology are potentially useful for studying LLMs.

What "recording" looks like in an LLM

In classical electrophysiology, you insert an electrode and record a spike train — a sequence of action potentials over time. In a modern large-scale recording experiment (e.g., silicon probes), you might record from hundreds or thousands of neurons simultaneously, capturing a population trajectory through high-dimensional state space as the animal performs a task.

In an LLM, the analog is to extract the residual stream activations at each layer. For a model with d dimensions and L layers processing a sequence of T input tokens, you obtain a tensor of shape T × L × d. Each token's trajectory through layers is a path through ℝd — a curve in high-dimensional space that starts at the token embedding and ends at the final residual state, which the unembedding maps to the pre-softmax logits. This is, literally, a population trajectory.

What's different from biological neuroscience

Advantages over biology
  • Full observability. Every dimension at every layer, every token. No sampling bias, no dropped spikes.
  • Zero noise. The same input gives identical activations. No trial-to-trial variability to average over.
  • Ground truth connectivity. We have the weight matrices. We know exactly how information flows — in principle.
  • Arbitrary intervention. Clamp, ablate, patch, or perturb any activation at any layer, any token, any dimension. Optogenetics with infinite precision.
  • Instant replay. Re-run the same prompt as many times as you want, with or without changes.
Challenges that remain
  • No clear "neuron" unit. Individual residual stream dimensions aren't interpretable on their own. Polysemanticity means features are distributed across dimensions.
  • No natural timescale. Layers aren't milliseconds. There's no oscillation, no theta rhythm, no obvious timescale to anchor the dynamics.
  • Training data is opaque. The model's "experience" (the training corpus) is vast, heterogeneous, and not under our control.
  • No obvious homolog. Which part of the LLM corresponds to hippocampus? Prefrontal cortex? It's unclear, and forcing the mapping may mislead.
  • Functional vs. mechanistic understanding. Knowing what a component computes doesn't tell us why it was necessary or how it generalizes.

Open questions

The analogy is productive but generates as many questions as it answers.

Key open questions
1
What is the right vocabulary? Neuroscience settled on tuning curves, population codes, attractors, and trajectories. Which of these transfer cleanly to LLMs? Which need modification? Which are actively misleading?
2
What counts as a "finding"? Discovering that V1 neurons respond to oriented edges is a finding because it constrains computational theories. What is the LLM equivalent? Does showing that mid-layer MLPs store factual associations constrain anything important?
3
How do we validate? Neuroscience validates by intervention and by behavior. LLMs allow arbitrary intervention — but what is the right behavioral read-out? Token probability? Task performance? Out-of-distribution generalization?
4
Do LLMs have "states"? Neural circuits pass through distinct dynamical states (rest, preparation, execution, recovery). Do LLMs have analogs? Do different prompt types produce qualitatively different trajectory shapes?
5
What's the right null model? When we see something interesting in neural data, we compare to a null (shuffled spikes, random connectivity). What's the null for an LLM's residual stream? A random transformer of the same size? A model trained on noise?
Learning across trials: A central interest in systems neuroscience is how population activity reorganizes across trials during learning. An analogous question exists for LLMs: how do residual stream dynamics change across training steps? This is the focus of developmental interpretability — a nascent subfield that has already found striking results, including sudden phase transitions in circuit formation (e.g., the abrupt emergence of induction heads) and late-stage generalization long after training loss converges (grokking). It remains underdeveloped relative to post-training analysis, largely because it requires model checkpoints saved throughout training. Few model families release them, but some do: Pythia (154 checkpoints per model) and OLMo.

Neuroscience parallels in Lessons 1–7

Lessons 1–7 compared language models with brains in many places, usually in a box at the end of a tab. Three patterns run through the table. Most parallels are structural (similar shapes of computation or representation), not claims of shared mechanism. Many break on the same few differences: brains are recurrent and learn continually with local rules, while a transformer runs one feedforward pass with frozen weights trained by backpropagation. Transformer Dynamics: A neuroscientific approach to interpretability of large language models
Fernando & Guitchounts · arXiv:2502.12131, 2025 · LLM residual stream as dynamical system

Background: the population dynamics framework

This paper applies a framework developed in systems neuroscience — the population dynamics view — to LLMs. The framework emerged from simultaneous recordings of many neurons in motor cortex and was synthesized in a landmark review (Vyas, Golub, Sussillo & Shenoy, Annual Review of Neuroscience, 2020). Rather than asking "what does this neuron encode?", it asks: "what shape does the population activity trace through high-dimensional space, and what does that shape reveal about the computation?"

When you record from N neurons simultaneously, you get a point in ℝN at each moment. The sequence of points over time is a trajectory. In practice, these trajectories tend to live on a neural manifold — a low-dimensional subspace capturing the computationally relevant variation. Motor cortex during reaching, for instance, uses only a handful of dimensions out of hundreds of recorded neurons.

Two concepts from this tradition are especially relevant here. First, attractors: regions of state space that the trajectory flows toward and stays near, arising from the learned connectivity of the network rather than any explicit programming. Second, rotational dynamics (Churchland et al. 2012): in motor cortex, population trajectories trace near-circular orbits when projected onto their top principal components, generating the sequential muscle activation needed for smooth reaching. Fernando & Guitchounts find rotational structure in Llama 3.1 8B as well — but in the phase portraits of individual units rather than in population principal components — and whether this reflects the same functional role remains an open question, since the transformer is feedforward rather than recurrent. In a transformer, "time" maps to layer depth, not to milliseconds; the geometric tools carry over, but their functional interpretation may not.

Overview

This paper directly imports the neuroscience dynamical systems framework into LLM analysis. The authors treat the residual stream of Llama 3.1 8B as a high-dimensional dynamical system and ask: what is the geometry of token trajectories across layers? The results are strikingly parallel to what has been found in motor cortex — cross-layer continuity, rotational structure, acceleration and deceleration phases, and a mid-network pseudo-attractor.

Methodology

Model

The primary model is Llama 3.1 8B (L = 32 transformer layers, hidden dimension D = 4,096). Gemma 2 was used in a preliminary analysis but results are not reported. The residual stream is extracted at two checkpoints per layer: before the attention sublayer's layer norm (hlAttn) and before the MLP sublayer's layer norm (hlMLP). This yields 2L = 64 interleaved sublayer states per sequence.

Tensor shape: B × 64 × 4096 where B = batch size, 64 = sublayer checkpoints, 4096 = residual stream dimension Each of the 4096 dimensions is referred to as a "unit."

Input data

Text is drawn from WikiText-2-raw-v1, a standard benchmark corpus of Wikipedia articles. Sequences are filtered to lengths between 100 and 500 characters. Each sequence is prepended with a beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token. All analyses are performed on the last token position only — the "readout" position that determines the next-token prediction. Most analyses use N = 1,000 sequences (the paper calls them both "samples" and "data batches"); the autoencoder (analysis 7) uses a separate, much larger set. A shuffled control condition randomly permutes all tokens after the BOS token, breaking syntactic and semantic structure while preserving token identity. A perturbation experiment uses the prompt "I'm sorry, Dave. I'm afraid I can't do that."

Analyses

1
Per-unit Pearson correlation. For each of the 4,096 units, the Pearson correlation of its activations across all samples is computed for every pair of sublayer checkpoints (l, m). This produces a distribution of correlations spanning all layer-pair combinations, showing how long individual unit activations remain stable as the stream evolves.
2
Cosine similarity between consecutive sublayers. CSl = (hl · hl+1) / (‖hl‖ ‖hl+1‖). Measures the angle between adjacent residual stream vectors — a value near 1 means the state barely changed direction; a lower value means a sharper turn in high-dimensional space.
3
Velocity. ‖Vl‖ = ‖hl+1 − hl‖₂ — the Euclidean distance between adjacent sublayer states. Where cosine similarity captures the direction of change, velocity captures its magnitude.
4
Phase portraits and rotation counting. For each unit i, the authors plot its activation ali (x-axis) against its layer-wise gradient (y-axis), forming a phase portrait. Trajectories are centered by subtracting the initial value. Tangent angles are computed with arctan2 and adjusted for ±π wrapping. The total rotation count per unit is R = (1/2π) Σ Δθl. A null distribution is generated by shuffling layer order 1,000 times per unit.
5
Mutual information (MI) between consecutive sublayers. MI is estimated per unit using Gaussian kernel density estimation, in nats. Formula: MI = Σ p(x,y) log( p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y)) ), with a small constant (10⁻¹⁰) added for numerical stability. Computed separately at attention → MLP and MLP → attention transitions to isolate the information-theoretic signature of each sublayer type.
6
PCA via SVD. The residual stream tensor (N × 2L × D) is reshaped to a (2NL) × D matrix and mean-centered. The top right singular vectors are the principal axes. Explained variance per component: rk = σk² / Σσi². Per-layer explained variance is also computed using the top 100 components.
7
Compressing autoencoder (CAE). To visualize trajectory geometry in 2D, a bottleneck autoencoder is trained: input dimension 4,096, bottleneck dimension 2, with hidden layers following a geometric width progression. Training uses Adam (lr = 10⁻³), MSE loss, up to 100 epochs with early stopping. The autoencoder was trained on 85k batches and tested on 15k batches, each of 64 samples.
8
Perturbation ("teleportation"). A 10 × 10 grid of points is spread across PC space and mapped back to activation space as x = z V2⊤ + μ (z: a grid point’s two PC coordinates; V2: the top two principal axes as columns; μ: the mean). These reconstructed activations replace the residual stream at chosen layer depths (layers 0, 7, 15, 23, 31), and recovery is measured as MSE against an unperturbed control over the following 12 sublayers. This tests whether the system has attractor-like dynamics — whether it pulls perturbed states back toward a canonical trajectory.

Finding 1: High cross-layer continuity (r > 0.8, rising with depth)

For each of the 4,096 units, its activations at adjacent sublayers are highly correlated across inputs: r > 0.8 even in the earliest layers, and rising with depth. The residual stream doesn't change drastically from layer to layer — each layer makes a relatively small modification to the existing state, and attention sublayers change it less than MLP-to-next-layer transitions. This is similar to the smooth population trajectories seen in motor cortex — abrupt discontinuities would suggest a phase transition or qualitative change in computation.

Finding 2: Velocity profile and attractor-like dynamics

Two related findings point to structurally distinct phases across the network.

Velocity profile. Velocity (‖hl+1 − hl‖₂) is roughly constant across early and mid layers, then rises, most steeply in the last third of the model. Note that cosine similarity — a separate measure — increases as a function of depth, meaning adjacent layers become more directionally aligned as you go deeper. The two measures are compatible: later layers can change the residual stream by a larger magnitude (higher L2 distance) while shifting its direction less abruptly (higher cosine similarity).

Attractor-like dynamics. The authors test whether the network has attractor-like dynamics by "teleporting" activations — replacing the residual stream at a chosen layer with points drawn from a grid in PCA space, then measuring how closely trajectories return to the unperturbed mean over the following 12 sublayers. Perturbations at the extremes fail: layer 0 produces highly variable dynamics; layer 31 causes "even more drastic" deviation. (The paper calls layer 31 "penultimate", but with 32 layers numbered from 0, as layer 0 implies, it is the last.) Mid-layer perturbations at layers 7, 15, and 23 show the most robust recovery. The abstract and section heading place the attractor-like dynamics in "lower layers"; the recovery results themselves point to layers 7–23. The Discussion characterizes this as a "pseudo-attractor" — the system tends to pull perturbed states back toward a canonical trajectory, but only when the perturbation occurs before the final output phase.

Finding 3: Rotational dynamics in single units (~10.74 rotations per unit)

This finding is about individual units, not the whole residual-stream vector. For each of the 4,096 residual-stream units, the authors draw a phase portrait: the unit's activation (x-axis, centered on its initial value) against its layer-to-layer change, its "gradient" over the 64 sublayer states (y-axis). They then count how many times the direction of travel along that 2D trajectory turns through a full circle (analysis 4 above).

On average, units complete about 10.74 rotations across the network (the mean over units, given in the paper's Fig. 2B caption). When the layer order is shuffled (1,000 shuffles per unit), the count drops to approximately zero, so the rotations depend on the order of the layers rather than arising by chance. The paper reports no spread (SD or range) and no test statistic for this comparison.

It is not the total angle that the whole residual-stream vector turns through. The paper never sums angles between consecutive whole-vector states; for the whole vector it reports cosine similarity and velocity instead (Finding 2).

Because residual-stream units are not a privileged basis, a unit's rotations need not reflect any rotation of the population state, unlike motor cortex, where rotations appear in the population's principal components; and the count of 10.74 depends on the 64 sublayer steps and on how the layer-to-layer "gradient" is estimated.

Open questions & future work

The paper's analyses all use a single input distribution — Wikipedia prose in English — and a single model (Llama 3.1 8B). This leaves several natural next steps open.

Stimulus variation. Do the dynamics look the same across different kinds of input? The paper does not compare RS trajectories for, say, English vs. French text, or formal vs. informal register; it defines a shuffled-token control but reports no results for it. The authors acknowledge this gap, noting that the low dimensionality they observe may partly reflect "the relative simplicity" of encoding high-probability Wikipedia sequences. Tasks requiring in-context learning or multi-step reasoning might reveal richer or qualitatively different trajectories.

Whole-sequence dynamics. All main analyses operate on the last-token position only. How representations evolve across the token dimension — not just the layer dimension — remains unexplored here.

Cross-model comparisons. A single model can't tell you whether the 10.74-rotation figure, the velocity acceleration pattern, or the attractor depth are universal or idiosyncratic. Comparing models of different scales, depths, and training objectives is needed to know whether these are architectural constants or incidental properties of one checkpoint.
